How to track contract renewal dates so none of them slip

Most companies record the wrong date. The renewal date is the one printed in the contract. The date that matters is the last day you can still act on it.

Why renewals slip

Almost nobody misses a renewal because they did not know the contract existed. They miss it because the deadline arrived while attention was somewhere else, and because the only reminder was in one person's head or one person's calendar.

Three patterns account for most misses. The contract was signed by someone who has since changed role, and the knowledge left with them. The reminder was set on the renewal date itself, by which point the notice window had already closed. Or the notice period was read as three months from today rather than three months before the end of a fixed term.

None of these are failures of diligence. They are failures of where the date was written down.

Record the notice date, not the renewal date

A renewal date tells you when something will have happened. A notice date tells you when you have to do something. Only the second is actionable, so it is the one that belongs in a list.

Calculate it once, at filing, and write it down as a date rather than as a rule. “Three months' notice to the end of the term” is a rule that has to be evaluated by whoever reads it. “Notice by 30.09.2026” is a fact that a calendar can hold.

Then set the reminder earlier than the notice date. Two to four weeks is usually right: enough time to find the file, ask whether the service is still worth having, get a signature, and send the letter by registered post if the contract requires it.

A worked example

A cleaning contract runs from 1 January 2025 for two years, renewing automatically for a further year unless terminated with three months' notice to the end of the term.

  • Term ends31.12.2026
  • Notice period3 months, to term end
  • Notice must arrive by30.09.2026
  • Reminder02.09.2026
  • If missed, next chance30.09.2027

The last row is the one to put in front of a decision maker. Missing 30 September does not cost a month. It costs a year of a contract you had decided to end.

Note also what “notice must arrive by” means. In most Swiss contracts, receipt counts, not posting. If the wording says notice must be received, a letter posted on 29 September has not been given in time. Where the contract is silent, ask a lawyer rather than assuming the friendlier reading.

The six fields worth keeping

Longer registers get abandoned. Six fields per contract is enough to act on, and short enough that people keep it up to date.

  1. Counterparty and subject. “Bühler AG, cleaning, Zürich site”. Enough to recognise without opening the file.
  2. Term end. The date the current period runs to.
  3. Notice date. Calculated, as a date.
  4. Annual value. So the list can be sorted by what is actually at stake.
  5. Owner. The person who decides, not the person who filed it.
  6. Link to the document. A path or a URL. Not “in the shared drive somewhere”.

Add the clause number the notice period came from if you have the patience. It saves the argument later about whether the three months were to term end or rolling.

Where to keep the list

A spreadsheet in the folder where the contracts live works. It is visible, everyone can read it, and it does not depend on anyone's inbox. Its weakness is that nothing forces an entry when a new contract is signed.

A shared calendar with the notice dates as all day events works as the reminder layer, but it is a poor register: you cannot sort it by value, and a deleted event leaves no trace.

SharePoint or Google Drive metadata columns are better, because the register lives on the document itself. See organising contracts in SharePoint for a column set that stays usable.

Whichever you choose, the constraint is the same: the register has to be updated at the moment of filing, by the person filing, or it decays. Every method that relies on someone returning to it later fails within a year.

One owner, one review a quarter

Name one person responsible for the register, usually whoever runs administration, and give them fifteen minutes a quarter to look at the next six months. Not to decide anything, just to check that every contract with a notice date inside the window has an owner who knows about it.

Also decide what happens when the owner is away. A notice date that lands during three weeks of summer holiday is the classic miss, and it is solved by a second name on the reminder, not by better intentions.

Four traps in the wording

Notice to term end versus rolling notice

“Three months' notice” on its own often means you can terminate at any time with three months' effect. “Three months to the end of the term” means one date a year. The difference is a whole contract year.

Automatic renewal for a different length

A two year contract that renews for one year, or for five, is common. Record the renewal length, not just the fact of renewal.

Written form and delivery

Some contracts require registered post, some accept email, a few require notice to a named address that has since changed. Check this at filing, not on the notice date.

Price increase windows

Many agreements let the supplier raise prices with notice, and give you a short right to terminate in response. That window is usually 30 days and it is not in your register unless you put it there.
