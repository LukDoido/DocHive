How long Swiss companies must keep documents

Ten years is the answer most people know. The useful questions are which documents it covers, what runs longer, and when the ten years start counting.

The ten year rule

The core obligation sits in Art. 958f of the Swiss Code of Obligations (OR). Books of account, accounting vouchers, the annual report and the audit report must be kept for ten years. The annual report and audit report must be kept in signed written form. The rest may be kept electronically, provided the conditions below are met.

This applies to every company required to keep books, which in practice means every entity in the commercial register, and sole proprietorships above the revenue threshold. There is no small company exemption from the ten years.

What counts as an accounting record

Broader than most people assume. An accounting voucher is any written record needed to verify a booking. Invoices and receipts obviously qualify. So do the documents behind them when the booking cannot be understood without them.

That pulls in a lot of what companies file loosely: supplier contracts that explain a recurring charge, leases behind rent bookings, insurance policies behind premium payments, loan agreements, and the calculations behind a work in progress valuation. Minutes of shareholder and board meetings have their own basis for retention, and in practice are kept permanently.

  • Books, vouchers, invoices10 years
  • Annual report, audit report10 years, signed written form
  • Contracts behind bookings10 years, from expiry
  • Records on immovable property20 years, VAT context
  • Board and shareholder minutesIn practice, permanently

Treat this table as a starting point for a conversation with your auditor, not as a determination. Sector rules, financial services, healthcare, construction, add their own periods on top.

When the clock starts

The ten years run from the end of the financial year, not from the date on the document. An invoice dated 3 February 2026, in a company with a calendar financial year, must be kept until the end of 2036.

For contracts, the practical reading is that the period runs from the end of the financial year in which the contract stopped having accounting effect, normally when it expired, not when it was signed. A ten year lease therefore stays relevant for twenty years in total. This is the single most common mistake in retention schedules, and it argues for deleting by review rather than by rule.

Where longer periods apply

VAT law extends retention to twenty years for documents connected with immovable property, because input tax adjustments can reach back that far. If you own, build or substantially renovate property, that is a separate class in your schedule.

Occupational pension records and certain insurance documentation follow their own rules and often outlive the ten years. Documents relevant to a dispute must be kept while the dispute is live regardless of any schedule, and destroying them once a claim is foreseeable is its own problem.

Guarantees and warranties are a quieter case. A five year construction guarantee is worth keeping for five years plus the limitation period that follows it, not because a rule says so, but because the document is the only evidence you have.

HR files, and the tension with data protection

Employment records pull in two directions. Payroll documents are accounting vouchers and follow the ten years. Social insurance records have their own periods, commonly five years and in some cases longer.

Meanwhile the revised FADP expects personal data not to be kept longer than necessary. Applicant files from a recruitment you closed are the clearest example: there is no accounting reason to keep them, and holding them for years is difficult to justify. Three to six months after the decision is the range usually recommended, unless the candidate agrees to longer.

Practical consequence: HR needs its own retention classes, separate from finance, and someone has to actually delete things. A schedule nobody executes is worse than none, because it documents an intention you did not follow.

Keeping records digitally

Electronic retention is allowed, and for most documents a scan is sufficient. The requirements, set out in the Ordinance on the Keeping and Retention of Business Records (GeBüV), are about integrity and availability rather than format: records must be unalterable or their alterations traceable, they must be readable for the whole period, and they must be findable within a reasonable time.

That last point is the one companies fail. A PDF on a shared drive satisfies integrity and readability. If nobody can locate the 2019 supplier agreement inside a reasonable time, the retention obligation has been met on paper and missed in substance, which is precisely the position auditors ask about.

Two documents still want their signed paper original: the annual report and the audit report. Keep a small physical archive for those and stop worrying about the rest.

What this means in practice

Three things are worth doing, in this order. Write a one page retention schedule with four or five classes and a period for each. Make sure filenames carry the date, so the age of a document is visible without opening it. Then set one annual review where documents past their period are deleted deliberately, by class.

Retention is usually treated as a storage question. It is a retrieval question. The obligation is not merely to have the document somewhere, it is to produce it when asked, years after the person who filed it has left.
